Nikola Tesla

The man who lit up the world

Here's a timeline of Nikola Tesla's life:

  • 1856 – Born in Smiljan, Austrian Empire (now Croatia)
  • 1875 – Enrolls at the Austrian Polytechnic in Graz to study engineering. Becomes obsessed with electricity and alternating current.
  • 1881 – Begins working for the Central Telephone Exchange in Budapest. First envisions the rotating magnetic field, a breakthrough for AC motors.
  • 1882 – Moves to Paris to work for the Continental Edison Company. Gains hands-on experience with electrical systems.
  • 1884 – Emigrates to the United States with little more than a letter of recommendation. Begins working for Thomas Edison in New York.
  • 1885 – Leaves Edison’s company after a dispute over AC vs. DC power and unpaid bonuses. Begins developing his own ideas for electrical systems.
  • 1887 – Develops and patents the AC induction motor and transformer. Forms the Tesla Electric Company.
  • 1891 – Invents the Tesla Coil, a high-voltage device still used in radio technology today. Also becomes a U.S. citizen.
  • 1893 – Demonstrates wireless transmission and alternating current at the Chicago World’s Fair. Helps Westinghouse win the contract to power the fair.
  • 1895 – Tesla’s lab in New York is destroyed in a fire, along with many of his notes, prototypes, and patents.
  • 1899 – Moves to Colorado Springs to experiment with wireless electricity and high-voltage transmission. Believes he has detected signals from extraterrestrial life.
  • 1901 – Begins building the Wardenclyffe Tower in Long Island, meant to transmit wireless electricity across the world.
  • 1904 – Funding for Wardenclyffe is pulled. The project is abandoned. Tesla becomes increasingly isolated and secretive.
  • 1917 – The Wardenclyffe Tower is demolished. Tesla continues inventing but lives in near poverty, increasingly ignored by the scientific community.
  • 1931 – Featured on the cover of Time magazine for his 75th birthday, briefly regaining attention and admiration.
  • 1943 – Dies alone in the New Yorker Hotel in New York City, in debt and largely forgotten by the public. Later that year, the U.S. Supreme Court credits him posthumously as the inventor of radio.
